# Introduction

## Rethinking Preservation

Preservation is one of humanity's oldest engineering challenges.

Long before electricity, refrigeration or modern manufacturing existed,
people sought ways to extend the life of food. Drying, fermentation,
smoking, salting and cooling each emerged from careful observation of
nature and a growing understanding of biological change. Although
separated by geography and culture, these methods shared a common
purpose: preserving food beyond the moment of harvest.

Today, preservation remains just as important.

Modern society depends on complex supply chains capable of transporting
food across continents while maintaining quality, safety and nutritional
value. As populations grow and resources become increasingly
constrained, the importance of efficient preservation continues to
increase.

Technological progress has transformed how preservation is achieved.
Refrigeration systems have become more efficient. Freeze dryers have
become more sophisticated. Sensors, automation and digital control
systems now monitor processes that once depended entirely on human
experience.

These developments represent remarkable engineering achievements.

Yet they also encourage a particular way of thinking.

When greater efficiency is required, the instinct is often to improve
the machine. More efficient compressors, better insulation, stronger
vacuum pumps and faster control algorithms all contribute to lower
energy consumption and improved performance.

These improvements are valuable.

However, they also raise a fundamental question.

**Are we improving the correct part of the system?**

This document proposes that preservation should not be viewed primarily
as a machine, a process or even a technology.

It should be viewed as a system.

A preservation system begins long before a product enters a freeze dryer
and continues long after it leaves one. Biological development,
cultivation methods, harvest timing, logistics, storage conditions,
software, energy availability and human decision-making all influence
the final outcome.

When these elements are considered independently, optimisation occurs
locally.

When they are considered together, optimisation becomes systemic.

This distinction is significant.

An efficient machine operating within an inefficient system can never
achieve the full potential of the system itself.

Conversely, a well-designed system often reduces the demands placed upon
every individual component.

The greatest improvements therefore do not always arise from building
better machines.

Sometimes they arise from asking better questions.

Could biology reduce the work required from engineering?

Could harvest strategies influence preservation energy?

Could software schedule preservation when renewable energy is abundant?

Could logistics shorten processing time before preservation begins?

Could preservation itself begin before harvest through a deeper
understanding of plant physiology?

Each question shifts attention away from individual components and
towards the relationships between them.

This document adopts that perspective throughout.

Its purpose is not simply to improve freeze drying.

Its purpose is to understand preservation as an interconnected
biological, physical and engineering system whose behaviour emerges from
the interaction of many smaller systems.

Only by understanding those relationships can meaningful optimisation
occur.

For this reason, the following chapters begin not with machines, but
with the fundamental processes they attempt to control.

Understanding should always precede optimisation.

Optimisation should always follow understanding.
