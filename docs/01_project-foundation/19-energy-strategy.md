# Energy Strategy

## Purpose

This document defines how energy should be understood, measured and managed throughout the Microgreens ERP project.

It provides strategic direction for cultivation, environmental control, preservation, storage, transport, computing and future expansion.

Energy efficiency is not the pursuit of the lowest possible electricity use.

The objective is to use energy where it creates lasting biological, operational and social value while preventing unnecessary demand, waste and dependence.

## Strategic Role

Energy connects every stage of the food chain.

It supports lighting, airflow, irrigation, sensing, computing, cooling, freezing, freeze drying, packaging, storage and transport.

A decision that reduces energy at one stage may increase waste or energy use elsewhere.

Energy must therefore be evaluated across the complete food chain rather than within isolated devices or processes.

## Energy Follows Biology

Biological requirements come before energy reduction.

Plants need suitable light, temperature, airflow, water and time.

Food preservation requires safe and reliable conditions.

People require safe equipment and suitable working conditions.

Energy savings must not undermine plant health, food safety, product quality or human responsibility.

Technology should adapt to biology, not biology to technology.

Engineering exists to support biological understanding, not replace it.

## Energy Hierarchy

Energy decisions should generally follow this order:

1. Avoid unnecessary demand.
2. Improve process design, layout and timing.
3. Recover or reuse available energy when safe and practical.
4. Select efficient and maintainable equipment.
5. Coordinate loads and reduce unnecessary peaks.
6. Consider renewable generation and storage after demand is understood.

Renewable energy does not remove the need for efficiency.

The least harmful energy demand is often the demand that careful design prevents.

## System-Level Evaluation

Energy performance should be judged by the useful result produced.

Relevant comparisons may include:

- energy per tray;
- energy per batch;
- energy per kilogram of fresh product;
- energy per kilogram of preserved product;
- energy per gram of usable finished product;
- crop yield and quality;
- preservation quality;
- shelf life;
- avoided food waste;
- avoided refrigeration;
- storage density;
- transport mass;
- equipment life;
- failed batches and rejected products.

A process that uses less electricity but produces more waste may be less efficient overall.

A process that uses additional energy may be justified when it protects quality, extends useful life or reduces larger demands elsewhere in the food chain.

## Waste Prevention and Resource Use

Preventing waste is part of energy management.

Every discarded seed, failed crop, spoiled product, damaged package or unnecessary transport movement contains energy that no longer creates useful value.

Energy waste should be reduced by:

- preventing avoidable crop failure;
- improving production planning;
- maintaining suitable storage conditions;
- detecting problems early;
- using appropriate batch sizes;
- reducing unnecessary handling;
- extending equipment life;
- repairing equipment when practical;
- avoiding premature replacement;
- reusing safe materials and recoverable energy;
- documenting unsuccessful processes.

The ERP should record energy lost through failed batches, spoilage and rejected products whenever the information can improve future decisions.

## Production and Preservation Planning

Fresh production should follow expected demand.

Preserved production should follow planned long-term use.

Production planning should consider:

- expected fresh demand;
- planned freeze-drying capacity;
- efficient batch size;
- existing preserved inventory;
- inventory turnover;
- shelf life;
- energy per batch;
- storage capacity;
- intended destination;
- long-term reserve requirements.

Surplus should not be created without purpose.

However, planned production for freeze drying is not wasteful overproduction when it extends useful life, improves batch efficiency and creates traceable long-term food reserves.

Freeze drying should therefore be planned as part of production rather than used only as a response to unexpected surplus.

## Cultivation Energy

Cultivation energy should be supplied according to actual biological requirements.

Useful daylight should be considered before artificial lighting when it can support safe and consistent growth.

Artificial lighting remains valuable when daylight is insufficient, variable or unsuitable.

Lighting efficiency depends on more than lamp efficiency.

Layout, height, distribution, reflection, timing, crop stage and heat production all influence the useful result.

Airflow, irrigation and environmental control should operate only when required, while remaining within safe biological boundaries.

Changes should be evaluated through crop response, yield, quality, uniformity and energy use.

## Preservation, Storage and Transport

Preservation may require substantial energy during processing while reducing energy and waste later.

Freeze drying should be evaluated using:

- energy per completed cycle;
- energy per dry kilogram;
- final product quality;
- shelf life;
- avoided spoilage;
- reduced product weight;
- storage requirements;
- packaging requirements;
- avoided refrigeration;
- transport efficiency;
- intended use.

A completed cycle does not by itself prove that energy was used effectively.

The product must remain safe, stable and useful.

Fresh, cooled, frozen and freeze-dried distribution should be compared according to the product, distance, storage period and intended destination.

## Energy Recovery

Waste heat and other recoverable energy should be identified throughout the system.

Recovery may be considered from:

- lighting;
- refrigeration;
- freezing;
- freeze-drying equipment;
- ventilation;
- computing equipment;
- other operational processes.

Recovery is valuable only when timing, temperature, distance and biological need align.

An energy recovery system should not introduce more complexity, maintenance or energy demand than the value it provides.

## Measurement and ERP Records

Energy should be measured at a level that supports decisions.

Useful measurement levels may include:

- whole-site consumption;
- major load groups;
- cultivation racks;
- freeze-drying cycles;
- cold storage;
- individual research trials;
- production batches.

The project should avoid installing a meter on every small device unless the information can change an action or decision.

ERP records should connect energy use with:

- equipment;
- operating period;
- batch identity;
- crop profile;
- environmental conditions;
- output;
- quality;
- waste;
- cost;
- maintenance;
- process changes.

Measurements require context.

A lower energy value is not automatically an improvement when output, quality or reliability also declines.

## Renewable Energy and Storage

Renewable generation and energy storage may strengthen sustainability and resilience.

Their suitability depends on:

- measured demand;
- demand timing;
- local conditions;
- available space;
- equipment life;
- material use;
- maintenance;
- replacement requirements;
- economic feasibility.

Generation and storage should be sized from evidence rather than ambition alone.

Reducing and coordinating demand should normally precede major investment in generation capacity.

Batteries should first protect functions that support safe control, data preservation, communication and orderly shutdown.

Maintaining every operational load during an outage may be unnecessary or uneconomic.

## Resilience and Critical Loads

Not every electrical load has equal importance.

Critical functions should be identified according to the consequences of interruption.

These may include:

- control systems;
- essential sensors;
- communication;
- data integrity;
- safe shutdown;
- alarms;
- equipment required to prevent immediate biological or product loss.

Backup power should protect justified critical loads.

After an interruption, equipment should return to a known and safe state.

Pumps, heaters, lighting and preservation equipment must not restart blindly.

The ERP should record interruption, recovery, missing measurements and affected batches.

## Responsible Scaling

Energy systems should scale according to demonstrated operational need.

Before expanding capacity, the project should understand:

- existing energy demand;
- major loads;
- peak demand;
- production output;
- equipment utilisation;
- waste;
- quality;
- maintenance requirements;
- opportunities for improvement.

Expansion should aim to reduce energy per useful unit whenever practical.

Larger equipment is not automatically more efficient when it operates with unsuitable or partly filled batches.

Standardised and modular systems should allow capacity to grow without losing traceability, safety or maintainability.

## Research and Continuous Improvement

Energy decisions should remain open to testing and revision.

Experiments may compare:

- lighting layouts;
- daylight integration;
- operating schedules;
- fan control;
- pump operation;
- insulation;
- heat recovery;
- cold-storage settings;
- freeze-drying profiles;
- batch loading;
- renewable generation;
- energy storage;
- demand coordination.

Each experiment should define its baseline, expected benefit and stopping criteria.

Successful and unsuccessful results should both be preserved.

Energy prices, equipment and available technologies change over time.

Assumptions should therefore be dated and reviewed periodically.

## Long-Term Direction

The long-term direction is an energy system that supports biological quality, food safety, resilience and responsible growth.

Energy demand should become more understandable as the project develops.

Waste should decline.

Equipment should remain useful for longer.

Preservation should extend the useful life of food.

Renewable generation and storage should be introduced where measured conditions justify them.

The objective is not to eliminate energy use.

It is to ensure that every justified unit of energy contributes as effectively as practical to useful food, preserved knowledge and a stronger food chain.
