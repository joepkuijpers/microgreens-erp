# Safety and Resilience Strategy

## Purpose

This document defines how safety and resilience should be incorporated throughout the Microgreens ERP project.

Safety prevents unnecessary harm.

Resilience enables the project to withstand disruption, recover responsibly and learn from what happened.

Together they protect people, food, biological systems, equipment, data, knowledge, communities and the environment.

## Relationship with Risk Management

Risk management identifies and evaluates uncertainty.

Safety and resilience determine how harm is prevented, how disruption is contained and how recovery is organised.

The two strategies support one another but have different purposes.

A risk may be accepted after review.

An unsafe condition must not be ignored merely because its likelihood appears low.

Human safety, food safety and legal obligations require suitable evidence and professional judgement.

## Safety Before Performance

Safety is a design requirement.

It is not an optional feature added after a system is built.

Efficiency, automation, production targets and financial benefit must not justify unacceptable harm.

When objectives conflict, the project should generally protect:

1. people;
2. food safety and product integrity;
3. biological systems and the environment;
4. critical data and knowledge;
5. equipment and production continuity;
6. efficiency and convenience.

The exact response depends on the situation, but immediate human safety comes first.

## Definition of Safety

Safety is the continuing protection of people, food, biological systems, equipment, information and the environment from avoidable harm.

Safety requires:

- suitable design;
- clear boundaries;
- competent operation;
- visible uncertainty;
- maintenance;
- monitoring;
- proportionate controls;
- responsible intervention;
- continuous learning.

Safety cannot be proven by the absence of previous incidents alone.

A system may remain dangerous even when nothing has yet gone wrong.

## Definition of Resilience

Resilience is the ability to anticipate disruption, limit its effects, recover essential functions and continue learning.

A resilient system is not one that never fails.

It is one that:

- makes failure visible;
- fails in a controlled manner;
- protects what matters most;
- preserves essential knowledge;
- supports responsible recovery;
- learns from disruption;
- becomes stronger where practical.

Resilience should not depend entirely on one person, device, supplier or undocumented procedure.

## Core Principles

The strategy follows these principles:

- Safety before efficiency.
- Prevention before correction.
- Understanding before automation.
- Monitoring before assumption.
- Visible uncertainty before false confidence.
- Safe failure before uncontrolled continuation.
- Recovery before unnecessary replacement.
- Learning before blame.
- Responsibility before convenience.
- Proportionate resilience before unnecessary redundancy.

These principles apply to strategic, technical and operational decisions.

## Responsibility and Authority

Safety is a shared responsibility.

Shared responsibility must not become unclear responsibility.

Every safety-critical process should identify:

- the responsible operator;
- the accountable owner;
- approved operating boundaries;
- required competence;
- escalation route;
- stop authority;
- recovery authority.

Anyone should be able to stop work when they reasonably believe that immediate safety or food integrity is threatened.

Stopping work should trigger assessment.

It should not automatically result in blame.

Only an appropriately responsible person should authorise return to service.

## Safety Domains

Safety should be considered across the complete project.

Relevant domains include:

- people and working conditions;
- food and product integrity;
- biological production;
- water and hygiene;
- chemicals and experimental inputs;
- electrical systems;
- mechanical equipment;
- heat, cold and pressure;
- software and data;
- automation and artificial intelligence;
- storage and transport;
- environmental and community effects;
- emergency response;
- knowledge continuity.

One event may affect several domains.

Controls should therefore be evaluated at system level.

## Hierarchy of Controls

Hazards should be controlled using the most effective practical method.

### Eliminate

Remove the hazard or unnecessary activity.

Examples may include removing an exposed electrical connection or eliminating an unsafe manual step.

### Substitute

Replace the hazard with a safer method, material or process.

A substitute must be evaluated for new risks.

### Isolate or Redesign

Separate people, food or biological systems from the hazard.

This may include guarding, physical separation, drainage, containment, electrical isolation or safer layout.

### Engineering Controls

Use designed controls that reduce exposure without depending entirely on memory.

Examples include:

- interlocks;
- current protection;
- pressure relief;
- safe-state control;
- automatic shutdown;
- physical barriers;
- suitable ventilation.

### Administrative Controls

Use procedures, training, schedules, signs, access restrictions and documented checks.

Administrative controls remain important but are less reliable when used alone.

### Personal Protective Equipment

Use suitable protective equipment for remaining exposure.

Protective equipment should not replace removal or redesign when stronger controls are practical.

Several layers may be required for critical hazards.

## Safe Design

Safe design begins before equipment is purchased or software is written.

Design review should consider:

- intended use;
- foreseeable misuse;
- operating environment;
- human interaction;
- biological variation;
- failure modes;
- maintenance access;
- cleaning;
- isolation;
- recovery;
- disposal;
- future modification.

Controls should remain understandable.

A safety feature that operators cannot recognise, inspect or test may create false confidence.

Complexity should be justified by the protection it provides.

## Graceful Failure

A system should move towards a known condition when it loses reliable information or control.

Graceful failure may require:

- automatic shutdown;
- limited continued operation;
- manual operation;
- isolation of one function;
- preservation of data;
- visible alarm;
- safe restart procedure.

Missing, stale, uncertain or conflicting data must remain visible.

An old measurement must never appear current.

Automation must not continue critical action merely because a communication channel failed silently.

Pumps, heaters, motors and preservation equipment must not restart blindly after interruption.

## Human Safety

Work should protect physical and mental wellbeing.

Relevant considerations include:

- electrical contact;
- moving equipment;
- sharp tools;
- lifting and repetitive strain;
- heat and cold;
- pressure and vacuum;
- slips and falls;
- hygiene products;
- experimental substances;
- fatigue;
- alarm overload;
- unsuitable working conditions.

Procedures should be realistic for the people performing the work.

Safety should not depend on perfect attention during every moment of routine operation.

Tasks with serious possible consequences require suitable training, supervision or specialist involvement.

## Food Safety and Product Integrity

Food must remain safe throughout cultivation, harvest, processing, preservation, packaging, storage and distribution.

Controls may include:

- approved inputs;
- hygiene;
- separation;
- traceable batches;
- suitable environmental boundaries;
- cleaning records;
- process validation;
- packaging integrity;
- storage requirements;
- product release criteria;
- controlled disposal.

Appearance, aroma or one sensor reading cannot prove that food is safe.

A completed preservation cycle does not by itself prove product stability.

Food-safety decisions should follow applicable law, validated methods and qualified professional advice where required.

Products should not be released when essential safety evidence is missing or unreliable.

## Biological Safety

Living systems vary and may respond unexpectedly.

Biological safety should consider:

- seed origin and quality;
- microorganisms;
- contamination;
- pests;
- allergens;
- plant toxicity;
- experimental species;
- environmental release;
- cross-contamination;
- disposal.

New crops and biological inputs should begin at a controlled scale.

Unknown material should not be introduced into routine food production without suitable assessment.

Experiments should remain clearly separated from approved production where their status or safety differs.

## Water and Hygiene

Water can support cultivation while also transferring contamination.

Water systems should consider:

- source;
- storage;
- contact materials;
- stagnation;
- cleaning;
- drainage;
- cross-connections;
- testing where justified;
- intended use.

Rainwater, reused water and experimental treatments require validation appropriate to their use.

Water that is suitable for plants is not automatically suitable for every food-contact purpose.

Cleaning methods should remove contamination without introducing unsuitable residues or damaging equipment.

## Electrical and Mechanical Safety

Electrical design should protect people, equipment and data.

Relevant controls include:

- suitable voltage and current ratings;
- protective devices;
- separation of water and electricity;
- secure connections;
- labelled isolation;
- strain relief;
- enclosure;
- grounding where required;
- accessible emergency disconnection.

Mechanical systems should address:

- moving parts;
- pinch points;
- instability;
- falling objects;
- unexpected movement;
- sharp edges;
- maintenance access.

Temporary prototypes require the same honest assessment as permanent systems.

Temporary does not mean exempt from safety.

Specialist electrical or mechanical work should be reviewed by suitably competent people.

## Heat, Cold, Pressure and Vacuum

Heating, cooling, freezing and freeze drying create hazards that may not be visible during normal operation.

Controls should consider:

- hot and cold surfaces;
- pressure difference;
- vacuum;
- refrigerants;
- stored energy;
- frost;
- condensation;
- ventilation;
- safe opening;
- maintenance isolation.

Equipment instructions and rated operating boundaries should be respected.

A process should not be modified beyond validated limits without suitable engineering review.

## Software and Data Safety

Software can influence physical and biological processes.

Data quality is therefore part of operational safety.

The system should distinguish between:

- observation;
- interpretation;
- warning;
- decision;
- command;
- outcome.

Software safety requires:

- validated inputs;
- bounded outputs;
- access control;
- change traceability;
- backups;
- recovery testing;
- clear timestamps;
- visible data quality;
- reversible changes;
- suitable audit history.

A successful command does not prove that the intended physical outcome occurred.

Where consequences matter, the outcome should be verified.

## Automation and Artificial Intelligence

Automation can repeat errors faster and for longer than a person.

Every safety-relevant automated function requires:

- defined purpose;
- approved boundaries;
- known failure modes;
- manual override;
- safe state;
- time limits;
- data-quality rules;
- responsible owner;
- recovery procedure;
- test evidence.

Artificial intelligence may support observation, analysis and recommendations.

It should not make unreviewed high-consequence decisions about human safety, food release or critical operating limits.

AI output must not hide uncertainty.

Human responsibility remains necessary.

## Monitoring and Alarms

Monitoring should make relevant change visible.

It should not create an illusion of control.

A monitoring system should show:

- current value;
- timestamp;
- source;
- status;
- uncertainty where relevant;
- missing or stale data;
- applicable boundary;
- required response.

An alarm should identify a condition that requires attention.

Too many low-value alarms can reduce response to important ones.

Alarm priority, ownership, acknowledgement and follow-up should be defined.

Critical alarms should be tested periodically.

## Resilience and Critical Functions

Not every function requires uninterrupted operation.

Critical functions should be identified according to the consequences of loss.

They may include:

- immediate human protection;
- food-safety controls;
- safe shutdown;
- essential sensing;
- critical communication;
- data preservation;
- environmental control needed to prevent rapid biological loss.

For each critical function, identify:

- acceptable interruption;
- detection method;
- backup or alternative;
- responsible person;
- recovery order;
- verification after recovery.

Resilience resources should protect justified functions first.

## Dependencies and Single Points of Failure

A process may depend on:

- one person;
- one supplier;
- one device;
- one database;
- one network;
- one power source;
- one undocumented method;
- one storage location.

Important dependencies should be visible.

A single point of failure should be removed, reduced or accepted explicitly according to its consequence.

Possible responses include:

- documentation;
- training;
- replacement parts;
- alternative suppliers;
- backups;
- manual procedures;
- functional separation;
- tested recovery.

The objective is not to duplicate everything.

Redundancy is valuable when it prevents greater loss than the resources and complexity it introduces.

## Backup and Recovery

A backup is useful only when it can support recovery.

Relevant backups may include:

- data;
- configuration;
- documentation;
- credentials;
- spare equipment;
- alternative procedures;
- supplier information;
- essential knowledge.

Recovery planning should define:

- what must be restored;
- priority;
- responsible person;
- required access;
- acceptable recovery time;
- verification;
- return to normal operation.

Backups and recovery procedures should be tested at a frequency appropriate to their importance.

## Emergency Response

An emergency response should prioritise:

1. protecting people;
2. stopping or isolating the hazard;
3. requesting suitable assistance;
4. protecting food and biological systems where safe;
5. communicating clearly;
6. preserving evidence where practical;
7. recovering through an approved process.

Emergency information should be accessible.

Relevant information may include:

- emergency contacts;
- isolation points;
- evacuation route;
- first-response instructions;
- equipment hazards;
- responsible persons;
- product containment requirements.

People should not be expected to improvise complex procedures during an emergency.

## Incidents and Near Misses

An incident is an event that caused harm, loss or significant disruption.

A near miss is an event in which harm was narrowly avoided.

Both provide evidence.

Records should include:

- what happened;
- when and where;
- people, products or systems affected;
- immediate response;
- available evidence;
- contributing conditions;
- underlying causes when understood;
- corrective action;
- responsible person;
- review date;
- wider lessons.

The objective is learning and prevention rather than blame.

Accountability remains necessary when procedures, responsibilities or ethical duties were ignored.

Repeated temporary corrections should trigger investigation of the underlying system.

## Return to Service

Equipment or processes should return to service only after suitable checks.

These may include:

- hazard removed or controlled;
- repair completed;
- cleaning completed;
- configuration confirmed;
- safety control tested;
- measurement validated;
- affected data reviewed;
- responsible approval;
- initial operation observed.

Restart should be controlled.

The absence of an alarm does not by itself prove normal operation.

## Training and Competence

People should understand the hazards, boundaries and responses relevant to their work.

Training should be:

- role-specific;
- understandable;
- practical;
- documented where important;
- refreshed when conditions change;
- tested through demonstration where appropriate.

Reading a procedure does not always demonstrate competence.

Critical tasks may require supervised practice or formal qualification.

Training should also explain when to stop and seek help.

## Documentation and Knowledge Continuity

Safety knowledge should not remain only in memory.

Relevant documentation may include:

- procedures;
- diagrams;
- equipment instructions;
- operating limits;
- maintenance history;
- incidents;
- recovery steps;
- emergency information;
- role assignments;
- decision records.

Documents should reflect actual practice.

Outdated safety documentation can be more dangerous than an acknowledged absence of information.

Critical knowledge should remain accessible during disruption while sensitive access remains protected.

## Relationship to the ERP

The ERP may support safety and resilience by connecting records across the system.

Relevant records may include:

- hazards;
- critical functions;
- equipment;
- responsible persons;
- operating boundaries;
- inspections;
- maintenance;
- training;
- incidents;
- near misses;
- corrective actions;
- affected batches;
- alarms;
- interruptions;
- recovery;
- return-to-service approval;
- review dates.

The ERP should preserve traceability.

It must not imply that completing a digital form proves that a physical condition is safe.

Verification remains necessary.

## Review and Continuous Improvement

Safety and resilience should be reviewed when:

- an incident or near miss occurs;
- equipment changes;
- software changes;
- a new crop or process is introduced;
- automation expands;
- production scale changes;
- responsibilities change;
- regulations change;
- recovery fails;
- the review date is reached.

Review should ask:

- Did the control work?
- Was the failure visible?
- Was the response timely?
- Was responsibility clear?
- Did recovery protect what mattered?
- Was unnecessary waste created?
- Which assumptions proved incorrect?
- What should change elsewhere?

Successes and failures should both strengthen future decisions.

## Long-Term Direction

The long-term direction is a food chain that protects people and food while remaining capable of learning and recovery.

Safety should become more deeply designed into each system.

Critical dependencies should become more visible.

Recovery should become faster and more reliable.

Knowledge should remain available beyond individual people or devices.

Growth should occur only when the project can manage the safety responsibilities it creates.

A resilient project does not hide failure.

It contains failure, learns from it and continues with greater understanding.
