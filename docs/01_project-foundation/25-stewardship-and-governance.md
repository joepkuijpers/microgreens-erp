# Stewardship and Governance

## Purpose

This document defines how the long-term purpose, integrity and continuity of the Microgreens ERP project should be protected.

It establishes who carries responsibility, which decisions require formal review and how governance should develop as the project grows.

Governance should support responsible action.

It should not introduce organisational complexity before real responsibilities require it.

## Stewardship Before Ownership

Stewardship is different from ownership.

Ownership may provide legal control over property, software, equipment or other resources.

Stewardship describes the responsibility to care for their purpose, condition and long-term effects.

A steward serves the project.

The project does not exist merely to serve the steward.

Stewardship requires decisions to consider:

- the mission;
- people;
- food safety;
- biological integrity;
- knowledge;
- natural resources;
- contributors;
- communities;
- future generations.

Legal ownership does not remove ethical responsibility.

Open access does not remove the need to protect project identity, trust and direction.

## Definition of Governance

Governance defines how authority, responsibility, review and accountability are organised.

It determines:

- who may decide;
- which decisions require review;
- which evidence is required;
- how conflicts are handled;
- how decisions are recorded;
- how authority is delegated;
- how continuity is protected;
- how governance itself may change.

Governance provides boundaries.

The Decision Framework provides the practical method used within those boundaries.

## Governance Principles

### Transparency

Important decisions should remain understandable.

The decision, reasoning, evidence, responsibility and relevant consequences should be documented at a level appropriate to their importance.

Transparency does not require unsafe or unlawful disclosure.

### Accountability

Every significant decision requires an identifiable person who accepts responsibility for the process and outcome.

Shared participation must not create invisible responsibility.

### Proportionality

Governance effort should match the importance, uncertainty and possible consequence of a decision.

Routine work should remain simple.

Foundational or high-risk decisions require stronger review.

### Traceability

Important decisions should remain connected to their evidence, project principles, affected systems and later results.

Future contributors should be able to understand why a decision was made.

### Competence

Authority should be supported by relevant understanding.

A person should not approve a specialised safety, legal, biological or technical decision merely because they hold a general leadership role.

Specialist advice should be sought when consequences exceed available knowledge.

### Participation

People affected by an important decision should have a suitable opportunity to provide information or concerns where practical.

Participation informs authority.

It does not make every decision a vote.

### Ethical Consistency

Convenience, urgency, funding or status must not silently override the project's ethical principles.

### Reversibility

Where practical, uncertain changes should begin small and remain reversible.

Irreversible decisions require stronger evidence and review.

### Long-Term Stewardship

Decisions should consider whether they strengthen or weaken the foundation inherited by future contributors and generations.

## Current Governance Stage

The project is currently founder-led.

Joep Kuijpers is the founder and accountable steward.

At this stage, one person may carry several roles because the project does not yet require a large governance structure.

This should be represented honestly.

The project should not claim to have councils, committees or independent review bodies that do not exist.

Current governance should therefore remain practical:

- routine work may proceed directly;
- significant decisions should be documented;
- specialist advice should be sought when needed;
- foundational changes require explicit founder approval;
- authority delegated to others should be clear;
- learning should remain visible.

Governance should grow with real responsibility, not with imagined organisational complexity.

## Governance Development

Governance may develop through stages.

### Founder-Led Stewardship

The founder carries final responsibility while the project remains small.

Consultation and external expertise may support decisions without creating fictional organisational roles.

### Delegated Responsibility

Specific responsibilities may be assigned to trusted contributors as operations, research and development expand.

Delegation should define scope, limits and reporting.

### Domain Stewardship

Experienced people may later become accountable for defined domains such as:

- biological production;
- food safety;
- engineering;
- software;
- research;
- data protection;
- finance;
- community relationships.

Domain authority does not automatically extend beyond the assigned area.

### Shared Governance

A broader governance structure may become appropriate when the project has several accountable leaders, legal obligations, employees, public funding or significant external impact.

Its form should be designed from actual needs and documented explicitly.

Progression between stages is not automatic.

A larger structure is justified only when it improves responsibility, knowledge and continuity.

## Decision Levels

Not every decision requires the same process.

### Routine Decisions

Routine decisions remain within approved procedures and have limited consequences.

They may be made by the person responsible for the work.

Examples include:

- normal scheduling;
- approved maintenance;
- routine data correction;
- established purchasing within agreed limits;
- minor documentation improvements.

Routine decisions should still remain traceable where operational records require it.

### Significant Decisions

Significant decisions may affect several processes, create meaningful cost, change an established system or introduce uncertainty.

They normally require:

- analysis;
- consultation where relevant;
- risk review;
- an accountable decision owner;
- a Decision Record;
- later validation.

### Foundational Decisions

Foundational decisions may change the project's identity, principles, ownership, long-term direction or essential responsibilities.

They require explicit approval from the accountable steward and clear documentation.

Future shared governance may require additional approval.

### Emergency Decisions

Emergency decisions may be necessary to protect people, food, equipment, data or continuity from immediate harm.

The responsible person may take proportionate temporary action without completing the normal process first.

The action, reason, consequence and follow-up must be documented as soon as practical.

Emergency authority must not silently become permanent authority.

## Reserved Decisions

Certain decisions require formal review because they may affect the foundation of the project.

Reserved decisions include material changes to:

- vision;
- mission;
- core values;
- ethics;
- governance;
- stewardship responsibilities;
- human flourishing principles;
- food-safety boundaries;
- data ownership and privacy;
- project identity;
- licensing of core project assets;
- ownership or transfer of essential resources;
- long-term strategic partnerships;
- major commercial dependence;
- automation with critical consequences;
- permanent discontinuation of essential knowledge or services.

A reserved decision should include:

- purpose;
- evidence;
- alternatives;
- affected principles;
- risks;
- responsible person;
- approval;
- implementation boundaries;
- review date.

Being reserved does not mean a decision can never change.

It means the change must be deliberate and visible.

## Roles and Responsibilities

### Accountable Steward

The accountable steward protects the project's purpose and approves foundational decisions.

The role includes responsibility for:

- alignment with vision and mission;
- governance integrity;
- responsible delegation;
- conflict escalation;
- continuity planning;
- foundational approval.

### Decision Owner

The decision owner coordinates a specific decision.

This person ensures that evidence, alternatives, consultation and consequences are considered.

### Reviewer

A reviewer examines reasoning, evidence, risk or alignment from a position sufficiently independent of the original proposal.

Review does not remove responsibility from the decision owner.

### Domain Specialist

A domain specialist contributes knowledge that general governance may not possess.

Specialist involvement is particularly important for:

- food safety;
- law and regulation;
- electrical safety;
- biological risk;
- data protection;
- finance;
- employment;
- public health.

### Contributor

A contributor may propose, test, document, review or implement improvements.

A valuable contribution deserves recognition.

It does not automatically create authority outside the contributor's agreed responsibility.

### Operator

An operator carries responsibility for approved operational work.

Operators should be able to stop or escalate work when safety, food integrity or approved boundaries are threatened.

One person may hold several roles while the project remains small.

The role performed in a particular decision should still be clear.

## Delegation of Authority

Authority may be delegated when another person has suitable knowledge, responsibility and access.

Delegation should state:

- the role;
- scope;
- permitted decisions;
- limits;
- reporting requirements;
- duration;
- review date;
- conditions for withdrawal.

Delegation transfers authority within its scope.

It does not remove the delegating person's responsibility to select and support the role responsibly.

Authority should be withdrawn or revised when competence, trust, availability or project needs change materially.

## Conflicts of Interest

A conflict of interest exists when personal, financial, professional or organisational interests may influence a project decision.

A conflict does not automatically imply misconduct.

Hidden conflicts weaken trust.

Relevant conflicts should therefore be disclosed before the decision is finalised.

Possible responses include:

- recording the interest;
- obtaining independent evidence;
- adding another reviewer;
- limiting participation;
- transferring decision authority;
- declining the proposal.

No person should approve a significant transaction solely on behalf of both sides when independent review is reasonably available.

Founder-led governance makes transparency especially important because several responsibilities may be held by one person.

## Open Collaboration

The project welcomes responsible participation.

Proposals should be evaluated by their:

- relevance;
- evidence;
- alignment;
- safety;
- maintainability;
- resource requirements;
- long-term consequences.

Decisions should not be based only on status, financial contribution, confidence or quantity of previous work.

Open-source access permits people to study, use and adapt material within the applicable licence.

It does not automatically grant authority to represent the official project or change its maintained direction.

Alternative versions and forks should be described honestly so users can distinguish them from the official project.

## Commercial Relationships

Commercial activity may provide resources for production, research, employment and responsible growth.

Funding and partnership do not purchase exemption from project principles.

Commercial relationships should be evaluated for:

- mission alignment;
- product and food integrity;
- financial dependence;
- supplier or customer concentration;
- data ownership;
- intellectual property;
- public representation;
- termination conditions;
- effects on local capability;
- long-term consequences.

Research results and operational evidence should not be altered to protect a commercial interest.

Material sponsorship or influence should be disclosed where relevant.

Short-term income should not create a dependence that prevents responsible future decisions.

## Project Identity and Representation

The project name, documentation and public claims affect trust.

People representing the project should distinguish between:

- verified project positions;
- approved plans;
- research findings;
- personal opinions;
- proposals;
- external adaptations.

Claims about food, health, sustainability, research or social impact require suitable evidence.

Public communication should not exaggerate current capability or imply approvals, certifications or partnerships that do not exist.

Corrections should be made transparently when material information proves inaccurate.

## Transparency and Confidentiality

Governance should be as open as responsibility allows.

Appropriate transparency may include:

- decision reasoning;
- project policies;
- research methods;
- material conflicts;
- significant changes;
- known limitations;
- correction history.

Some information requires protection, including:

- personal data;
- security-sensitive details;
- confidential contracts;
- credentials;
- legally protected information;
- information whose release could create food-safety or operational harm.

Confidentiality should protect legitimate interests.

It should not be used to hide misconduct, unsafe practice or misleading claims.

## Disagreement and Escalation

Disagreement can improve decisions when it reveals assumptions or missing evidence.

Concerns should be addressed through:

1. clarification of the issue;
2. collection of relevant evidence;
3. identification of affected principles and responsibilities;
4. review by suitable people;
5. an accountable decision;
6. documentation where significant.

A person should be able to escalate concerns involving:

- immediate safety;
- food integrity;
- unlawful conduct;
- serious ethical conflict;
- concealed data loss;
- misuse of project identity;
- abuse of authority;
- retaliation for responsible reporting.

Disagreement should focus on evidence, reasoning and consequences rather than personal status.

## Emergency Governance

Immediate action may be required when delay could cause serious harm.

Emergency action should:

- address the immediate threat;
- remain proportionate;
- preserve evidence where practical;
- protect people first;
- avoid unnecessary secondary harm;
- notify the accountable person;
- be reviewed afterwards.

An emergency decision should be temporary unless the normal governance process later confirms it.

A post-event review should identify:

- what happened;
- why normal governance was insufficient;
- which actions were taken;
- what consequences followed;
- what should change.

## Governance Records

Important governance records may include:

- Decision Records;
- delegated authorities;
- role assignments;
- approvals;
- conflicts of interest;
- policy changes;
- emergency actions;
- reviews;
- succession arrangements;
- amendments.

Records should remain connected to relevant project references, evidence, risks and affected systems.

The objective is not to document every conversation.

The objective is to preserve the reasoning and responsibility behind decisions that matter.

## Relationship to the ERP

The ERP may eventually support governance through controlled records and workflows.

It may record:

- governance identifier;
- decision owner;
- responsible steward;
- reviewers;
- affected domains;
- required approvals;
- status;
- evidence;
- conflicts of interest;
- decision;
- conditions;
- review date;
- superseding decision;
- audit history.

The ERP should support human accountability.

It must not automatically approve foundational, ethical or high-consequence decisions.

Automated tools may identify missing information, inconsistent references or required reviews.

Final responsibility remains human.

## Succession and Continuity

The project's knowledge, access and responsibility should not depend permanently on one person's availability.

Continuity planning should gradually protect:

- repository access;
- documentation;
- credentials;
- operational knowledge;
- legal and financial records;
- supplier and partner information;
- safety responsibilities;
- essential decision history.

Sensitive access should remain protected while still permitting responsible recovery.

A future steward should be selected according to:

- understanding of the project;
- demonstrated integrity;
- relevant competence;
- willingness to serve its purpose;
- respect for evidence;
- ability to collaborate;
- long-term responsibility.

Succession should transfer responsibility, not personal ownership of the project's purpose.

Future stewards may develop the project as knowledge and circumstances change.

They should not quietly rewrite its foundation for short-term private advantage.

If no suitable successor exists, preservation of knowledge and safe reduction of activities may be more responsible than uncontrolled continuation.

## Governance Review

Governance should be reviewed when:

- responsibilities change;
- contributors or employees join;
- legal structure changes;
- significant funding is accepted;
- operations expand;
- food-safety obligations change;
- several people receive decision authority;
- a governance failure occurs;
- succession becomes necessary;
- the review date is reached.

Review should ask:

- Does authority match responsibility?
- Are decisions proportionate and traceable?
- Are important conflicts visible?
- Can concerns be escalated?
- Is specialist knowledge used appropriately?
- Is governance helping or obstructing responsible work?
- Does continuity depend excessively on one person?
- Are foundational principles still protected?

## Amendment of Governance

This document may evolve.

A material amendment should explain:

- the problem;
- proposed change;
- evidence;
- affected responsibilities;
- risks;
- alternatives;
- approval;
- effective date;
- future review.

Previous governance decisions and document versions should remain preserved through version control and Decision Records.

Changes should strengthen governance rather than merely concentrate convenience or authority.

## Long-Term Direction

The long-term direction is a project guided by responsible stewardship rather than personal control alone.

Authority should remain connected to knowledge and accountability.

Open collaboration should remain possible.

Important decisions should remain traceable.

Commercial activity should support rather than redefine the mission.

Governance should develop only when real people, responsibilities and consequences require it.

The project should be able to continue beyond its founder without losing the knowledge, integrity and purpose upon which it was built.
