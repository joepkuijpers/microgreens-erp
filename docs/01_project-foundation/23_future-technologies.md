# Future Technologies

## Purpose

This document defines how the Microgreens ERP project should explore, evaluate, adopt and retire emerging technologies.

The project should remain open to useful innovation without allowing novelty to determine its direction.

Future technologies are valuable when they improve biological understanding, food quality, safety, accessibility, resilience or responsible resource use.

## Strategic Role

Technology changes rapidly.

The purpose of a long-term strategy is therefore not to predict which devices or platforms will succeed.

It is to establish principles that remain useful while available tools change.

A future technology should strengthen the food chain and the people who understand and maintain it.

It should not create unnecessary complexity, waste or dependence.

Technology should adapt to biology, not biology to technology.

Engineering exists to support biological understanding, not replace it.

## Problems Before Technology

Exploration should begin with a real biological, operational or social problem.

Before testing a technology, ask:

- Which problem does it address?
- Who experiences the problem?
- What is the current method?
- What evidence describes the current situation?
- Could a simpler solution provide sufficient value?
- What happens if no change is made?
- What new problems may the technology create?

A product demonstration or technical possibility is not by itself a reason for adoption.

The project should avoid searching for problems merely to justify an attractive technology.

## Technology Assessment

A candidate technology should be assessed across its complete life cycle.

Relevant questions include:

- Does it improve biological understanding?
- Does it improve safety, quality or traceability?
- Does it reduce avoidable waste?
- Does it save meaningful time or repetitive labour?
- Does it reduce energy, water or material demand?
- Can the claimed benefit be measured?
- Can people understand its operation?
- Can it be overridden or operated manually?
- Is it maintainable and repairable?
- Are replacement parts available?
- Does it use open formats and documented interfaces?
- Who owns the data?
- Does it require continuous external connectivity?
- Does it create dependence on one supplier or platform?
- What are its purchase, operating and replacement costs?
- What happens when it fails or becomes unsupported?
- Can it be removed without damaging the wider system?

The assessment should consider benefits, risks, uncertainty and opportunity costs.

Choosing one technology may prevent investment in a simpler or more valuable improvement.

## Small and Reversible Experiments

A future technology should begin with the smallest useful experiment.

The experiment should be limited enough to control exposure while still producing meaningful evidence.

Before testing begins, define:

- the problem;
- current baseline;
- proposed benefit;
- test scale;
- measurements;
- comparison method;
- success criteria;
- failure criteria;
- stop conditions;
- maximum duration;
- responsible person;
- recovery or removal procedure.

Where practical, the change should remain reversible.

A technology that cannot be tested safely at a small scale requires stronger evidence and review before use.

## Evidence Before Adoption

Adoption should follow demonstrated value.

Evidence may include:

- biological response;
- product quality;
- food safety;
- measurement reliability;
- time saved;
- reduced physical strain;
- energy use;
- water use;
- material use;
- avoided waste;
- maintenance demand;
- equipment life;
- operating cost;
- user understanding;
- failure behaviour.

A successful technical demonstration does not prove that a technology is suitable for routine operation.

The result must remain useful under realistic conditions and over a meaningful period.

Negative results should be preserved.

They prevent repeated investment in weak ideas and help future contributors understand why a technology was not adopted.

## Biology and Human Understanding

Technology should increase the ability to observe and understand living systems.

It should not hide biological reality behind automated outputs.

A measurement requires context.

A recommendation requires explanation.

An automated action requires approved boundaries and human responsibility.

People should retain practical experience with the underlying process whenever possible.

Technology should support craftsmanship, curiosity and judgement rather than make them unnecessary.

## Open and Maintainable Technology

The project should prefer technology that remains understandable and maintainable.

Useful characteristics include:

- open data formats;
- documented interfaces;
- exportable records;
- replaceable components;
- repairable construction;
- local configuration;
- offline operation where practical;
- manual override;
- clear failure behaviour;
- active and transparent maintenance.

Proprietary technology may still be considered when it provides clear value and acceptable conditions.

Its dependencies, data access, replacement options and exit path should be documented before adoption.

A system should not become essential before the project understands how it can be maintained or replaced.

## Resource Use and Waste Prevention

Future technology should be evaluated by the resources it consumes as well as the value it creates.

Its life-cycle effects may include:

- raw materials;
- manufacturing;
- transport;
- electricity;
- consumables;
- software subscriptions;
- maintenance;
- replacement parts;
- batteries;
- electronic waste;
- disposal.

A more advanced device is not automatically more sustainable.

Where practical, technology should help:

- detect failing crops earlier;
- reduce avoidable overproduction;
- improve preservation planning;
- extend product shelf life;
- prevent energy and material loss;
- reduce rejected batches;
- identify inefficient equipment;
- support repair before replacement;
- extend equipment life;
- reuse suitable components;
- preserve negative research results.

Additional measurement is justified when it can improve a decision.

Collecting data without a clear purpose may create unnecessary equipment, storage, energy use and maintenance.

## Candidate Technology Areas

Candidate areas indicate possible directions for investigation.

They are not approved purchases or commitments.

Potential areas include:

- biological and environmental sensing;
- imaging and computer vision;
- portable laboratory methods;
- spectroscopy;
- low-power rack nodes;
- local and distributed computing;
- robotics for repetitive handling;
- artificial intelligence;
- energy recovery and storage;
- demand coordination;
- advanced preservation;
- improved packaging;
- local manufacturing;
- resilient communication systems.

Candidate areas should change as evidence, needs and available technology develop.

## Sensors and Biological Observation

Future sensors may improve understanding of plants, water, air, light, equipment and preserved products.

Possible developments include:

- improved light measurement;
- plant temperature sensing;
- gas and air-quality measurement;
- water-quality observation;
- non-destructive moisture assessment;
- product stability measurement;
- portable spectroscopy;
- improved calibration methods.

A sensor is useful only when its measurement is reliable enough to support a decision.

Calibration, drift, placement, maintenance and uncertainty must remain visible.

Sensors should not be added merely because a value can be measured.

## Imaging and Computer Vision

Imaging may support observation of:

- germination;
- plant spacing;
- canopy development;
- crop uniformity;
- colour;
- visible stress;
- contamination;
- harvest readiness;
- product appearance.

Camera position, lighting, crop stage and image quality should be recorded.

Computer vision may suggest patterns, but its results require validation against direct observation.

It must not claim to observe biological features that are not visible in the image.

Images and derived results should remain linked to their source, method and review status.

## AI, Automation and Robotics

Artificial intelligence may help compare records, identify patterns, analyse images and suggest explanations.

Its output should remain linked to:

- source data;
- model or method;
- version;
- confidence or uncertainty;
- human review;
- resulting decision.

Automation may act only within tested and approved boundaries.

Missing, stale or conflicting information should reduce confidence and lead towards a safe state.

Robotics may support repetitive, physically demanding or highly consistent tasks.

Before adoption, robotics should demonstrate that it improves the complete process without creating disproportionate maintenance, safety risk, energy use or dependence.

Humans remain responsible for important biological, operational and ethical decisions.

## Energy, Preservation and Logistics

Future technologies may improve how energy, preservation, storage and transport work together.

Candidate developments include:

- more efficient lighting layouts;
- adaptive environmental control;
- heat recovery;
- thermal storage;
- renewable generation;
- battery storage;
- coordinated electrical demand;
- improved freezing;
- efficient freeze-drying cycles;
- non-destructive endpoint measurement;
- lower-impact packaging;
- improved storage monitoring;
- route and inventory coordination.

Evaluation should consider the complete result.

A preservation method may require energy while reducing spoilage, refrigeration, storage volume and transport mass later.

Technology should be judged by useful food, preserved quality and avoided waste rather than by machine efficiency alone.

## Communication and Distributed Systems

Communication systems support monitoring, coordination, traceability and emergency response.

The project may investigate:

- local networks;
- low-power wireless communication;
- mesh networks;
- long-range radio;
- store-and-forward messaging;
- offline-first applications;
- local data synchronisation;
- independent alert channels.

These systems may strengthen operations where internet service is unavailable, unreliable or inappropriate.

They should not be described as internet replacement when they provide only limited messaging or data transfer.

Communication choices must consider legality, range, power use, security, maintenance and actual operational need.

Critical local operation should not depend unnecessarily on continuous access to an external cloud service.

## Local Capability and Accessibility

Technology should strengthen local capability.

Where practical, systems should be:

- affordable;
- understandable;
- repairable;
- adaptable;
- documented;
- usable with locally available skills and components.

Local manufacturing, open designs and modular hardware may reduce dependence and support knowledge sharing.

However, local production is not automatically better when quality, safety or total resource use becomes worse.

Technology transfer should include knowledge, limitations, maintenance and responsible use.

The objective is empowerment rather than permanent dependence on imported equipment or hidden expertise.

## ERP Technology Register

The ERP should eventually maintain a traceable technology register.

A record may include:

- identifier;
- technology name;
- problem addressed;
- responsible person;
- current status;
- proposed benefit;
- baseline;
- supplier or source;
- hardware and software dependencies;
- data ownership;
- open or proprietary interfaces;
- energy and material requirements;
- maintenance requirements;
- estimated life;
- replacement or exit path;
- linked experiments;
- evidence;
- risks;
- costs;
- decision;
- review date;
- lessons learned.

The register should connect candidate technologies with research, equipment, risks, maintenance, costs and operational results.

It should support decisions rather than become a catalogue of products.

## Technology Status

A technology may use one of four principal statuses.

### Candidate

The technology may address a relevant problem but has not yet been tested sufficiently.

### Experiment

The technology is being evaluated within defined boundaries.

### Approved

Evidence shows sufficient value, risk is acceptable and maintenance is realistic for its intended use.

Approval should describe the specific use and boundaries.

### Retired

The technology is no longer used because it became unsuitable, unsupported, unsafe, unnecessary or inferior to another approach.

A retired technology should retain its records and lessons.

Status should never be inferred only from whether equipment is physically present.

## Adoption and Scaling

Adoption requires more than a successful experiment.

Before routine use or scaling, confirm:

- demonstrated benefit;
- acceptable residual risk;
- repeatable operation;
- realistic maintenance;
- available skills;
- traceable data;
- justified cost;
- manageable dependencies;
- safe failure;
- recovery procedure;
- compatibility with the wider system.

Scaling should occur gradually.

A technology that succeeds on one rack or batch may behave differently across a larger operation.

Expansion should remain measurable and reversible where practical.

## Retirement and Replacement

Technology should be reviewed throughout its useful life.

Retirement may be appropriate when:

- support ends;
- security can no longer be maintained;
- measurements become unreliable;
- repair becomes impractical;
- operating cost becomes disproportionate;
- a simpler solution provides equal value;
- the original problem no longer exists;
- unacceptable dependence develops.

Retirement should protect data, documentation and operational continuity.

Equipment should be reused, repaired, repurposed or recycled responsibly where practical.

Replacement should solve a demonstrated need rather than follow a routine upgrade cycle.

## Review and Learning

Future technology decisions should be reviewed when:

- new evidence becomes available;
- an experiment ends;
- a failure or near miss occurs;
- costs change;
- support conditions change;
- a supplier changes ownership or access;
- regulations change;
- better alternatives become available;
- the review date is reached.

Reviews should compare the technology with the current baseline and its original purpose.

Assumptions should be dated because technology, prices and project needs change.

## Long-Term Direction

The long-term direction is a project that remains technologically capable without becoming technology-led.

New tools should deepen biological understanding.

Automation should remain accountable.

Data should remain accessible.

Systems should remain maintainable and replaceable.

Innovation should reduce waste, strengthen resilience and expand responsible local capability.

A future technology earns adoption through demonstrated value, not novelty.
