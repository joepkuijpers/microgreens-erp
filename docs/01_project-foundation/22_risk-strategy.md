# Risk Strategy

## Purpose

This document defines how risks and uncertainties should be identified, assessed, treated and reviewed throughout the Microgreens ERP project.

Risk management supports responsible decisions without preventing experimentation, learning or innovation.

The objective is not to remove every uncertainty.

It is to understand what may affect the project, protect what matters and make deliberate choices with clear responsibility.

## Role of Risk Management

Every activity involves uncertainty.

Seeds may perform differently than expected.

Equipment may fail.

Measurements may be incomplete.

Suppliers may become unavailable.

Costs, regulations and environmental conditions may change.

New technology may create opportunities while introducing dependency or complexity.

Risk management makes these uncertainties visible before they become uncontrolled problems.

It connects strategy, safety, research, engineering, operations and governance.

## Risk Is Part of Learning

Uncertainty is not automatically a reason to avoid action.

Experiments, new crops, preservation methods and technical improvements require controlled exposure to uncertainty.

Risk management should support responsible action, not prevent learning through excessive caution.

A small, reversible and well-observed experiment may carry less long-term risk than continuing an inefficient or poorly understood process.

The project should distinguish between:

- uncertainty that requires investigation;
- manageable risk that permits controlled action;
- unacceptable risk that requires prevention or immediate intervention;
- opportunity that deserves careful exploration.

## Risk Categories

Risks should be considered across the complete food chain and project structure.

Relevant categories include:

- human safety;
- food safety and product integrity;
- biological production;
- equipment and infrastructure;
- energy and environmental control;
- water and hygiene;
- data, software and cybersecurity;
- automation and artificial intelligence;
- suppliers and logistics;
- financial continuity;
- legal and regulatory compliance;
- reputation and public trust;
- environmental and social impact;
- strategic dependence;
- knowledge continuity;
- missed opportunities.

Categories help organise attention.

They should not be treated as isolated areas because one event may affect several parts of the system.

## Identification Before Assessment

A risk should be described clearly before it is scored or treated.

A useful description explains:

- the uncertain event or condition;
- its possible cause;
- what it may affect;
- the possible consequence;
- existing controls;
- available evidence;
- important assumptions.

Vague statements such as “equipment risk” provide little guidance.

A clearer description is:

> If the irrigation pump stops during an unattended period, trays may lose water before the failure is noticed, causing crop loss and an incomplete batch record.

Clear descriptions support useful action and later learning.

## Likelihood and Consequence

Risks should be assessed using the simplest method that supports a decision.

### Likelihood

- **Unlikely:** not expected during normal operation, but still possible.
- **Possible:** may occur under realistic operating conditions.
- **Likely:** expected to occur or has occurred repeatedly.

### Consequence

- **Limited:** manageable disruption with little lasting harm.
- **Serious:** meaningful effect on safety, quality, production, cost, trust or continuity.
- **Critical:** potential for severe harm, unsafe food, major loss, legal failure or long-term project damage.

Assessment should use evidence where available.

When evidence is weak, uncertainty should be stated rather than hidden behind a precise score.

## Risk Priorities

Priority determines how quickly and formally a risk should be addressed.

- **Low:** monitor through normal work.
- **Medium:** assign an action and review date.
- **High:** reduce exposure before expansion or routine use.
- **Urgent:** stop or control the activity immediately.

A critical consequence may require a high or urgent priority even when likelihood appears low.

Human safety, food safety and legal obligations must not be reduced to a simple numerical score without professional judgement.

## Risk Responses

A risk may require one or more responses.

### Avoid

Remove the activity or condition when the potential harm is unacceptable and no responsible control is available.

### Reduce

Lower likelihood or consequence through design, training, monitoring, maintenance, testing or operational controls.

### Prepare and Recover

Accept that interruption may occur while establishing detection, backup, safe failure and recovery procedures.

### Transfer or Share

Use insurance, contracts, specialist services or partnerships where another party can manage part of the exposure more appropriately.

Responsibility cannot be transferred blindly.

### Accept

Accept a risk when its exposure is understood, proportionate and justified.

Acceptance must be explicit for significant risks.

### Investigate

Collect evidence before deciding when uncertainty is too high for responsible assessment.

### Pursue Carefully

Treat an opportunity as a controlled experiment when its potential value justifies limited and reversible exposure.

## Residual and Accepted Risk

Controls rarely remove all risk.

The exposure remaining after controls is the residual risk.

For significant risks, the project should record:

- which controls are active;
- which uncertainty remains;
- why the residual risk is acceptable;
- who accepted it;
- when the decision must be reviewed.

Risk acceptance is not the same as ignoring a problem.

It is a documented decision based on available evidence, alternatives and consequences.

Temporary acceptance should include an expiry or review date.

## Experiments and Stop Conditions

Experiments require freedom to learn and boundaries that prevent uncontrolled harm.

Before an experiment begins, define:

- the question being tested;
- the smallest useful scale;
- expected benefit;
- known hazards;
- uncertain factors;
- measurements;
- success criteria;
- failure criteria;
- stop conditions;
- recovery or disposal procedure;
- responsible person;
- maximum duration or exposure.

Stop conditions should be observable and practical.

An experiment should stop when safety, food integrity, biological welfare, equipment condition or data reliability moves outside approved boundaries.

Stopping an experiment is not automatically failure.

It may be the correct result of responsible observation.

## Automation and Data Risk

Automation can repeat both good and poor decisions at scale.

Before automation is authorised, the project should understand:

- the process;
- input data;
- operating boundaries;
- expected outcome;
- failure modes;
- manual override;
- safe state;
- recovery procedure;
- responsible person.

Missing, stale, uncertain or conflicting data must remain visible.

Old measurements must never appear current.

Automation should reduce confidence or move towards a safe state when its evidence becomes unreliable.

Artificial intelligence may support analysis and recommendations.

It must not hide uncertainty or remove human responsibility for important decisions.

Changes to software, configuration and automated rules should remain traceable and reversible.

## Food and Biological Risk

Living systems vary.

Seed quality, germination, climate, water, microorganisms and handling can influence results even when procedures appear unchanged.

Biological risk should be managed through:

- suitable input controls;
- hygiene;
- traceable batches;
- environmental observation;
- defined process boundaries;
- quality checks;
- separation where contamination is possible;
- documented disposal decisions;
- investigation of repeated variation.

Food safety decisions require appropriate evidence and legal compliance.

Appearance, aroma or an automated reading alone cannot prove that a product is safe.

Preservation must achieve verified product stability rather than merely complete a programmed cycle.

## Operational and Supply Risk

Operations depend on people, equipment, materials, services and suppliers.

Relevant risks include:

- equipment failure;
- unavailable replacement parts;
- loss of power or communication;
- unsuitable maintenance;
- single-source dependency;
- delayed seed or packaging supply;
- transport disruption;
- insufficient storage;
- incomplete procedures;
- loss of essential knowledge.

Critical dependencies should be identified before capacity expands.

Where practical, the project should use maintainable systems, interchangeable components, documented procedures and justified alternatives.

Redundancy should protect important functions without creating unnecessary equipment, cost or waste.

## Strategic, Financial and Social Risk

A technically successful process may still create strategic, financial or social harm.

Decisions should consider:

- affordability;
- cash flow;
- long-term operating cost;
- dependence on one product, customer or supplier;
- dependence on proprietary technology;
- environmental burden;
- workforce capability;
- public trust;
- effects on local producers;
- unintended long-term dependence.

Growth should not proceed faster than the project can understand, finance, maintain and govern responsibly.

Supporting communities should strengthen local capability rather than replace it.

Transparency must protect trust without exposing personal, confidential or security-sensitive information.

## Missed Opportunities

Risk management must also consider the consequences of doing nothing.

Excessive caution may delay:

- useful research;
- waste reduction;
- safer processes;
- improved food preservation;
- local knowledge sharing;
- responsible automation;
- better resource efficiency.

An opportunity should be assessed by its possible value, uncertainty, reversibility and learning potential.

Small trials can preserve opportunity while limiting exposure.

## Risk Ownership and Escalation

Every significant risk requires an owner.

The owner is responsible for:

- maintaining the risk description;
- checking evidence;
- coordinating actions;
- monitoring controls;
- reporting changes;
- arranging review.

Ownership does not mean one person performs every action.

Risks should be escalated when:

- consequences may be critical;
- controls fail;
- exposure moves outside approved boundaries;
- the responsible person lacks authority or expertise;
- several systems or stakeholders are affected;
- legal or food-safety requirements may be involved;
- accepted risk is no longer justified.

Important decisions should involve the people with the knowledge and authority required to make them responsibly.

## ERP Risk Register

The ERP should eventually provide a traceable risk register.

A risk record may include:

- identifier;
- title and description;
- category;
- cause;
- possible consequence;
- affected process, equipment, product or batch;
- likelihood;
- consequence;
- priority;
- existing controls;
- selected response;
- required actions;
- owner;
- target date;
- residual risk;
- acceptance decision;
- review date;
- linked incidents;
- supporting evidence;
- lessons learned;
- status.

Risk records should connect with maintenance, batches, equipment, suppliers, research, corrective actions and operational decisions where relevant.

The register should support decisions rather than become an administrative collection of inactive entries.

## Incidents and Near Misses

An incident shows that an unwanted event occurred.

A near miss shows that harm was narrowly avoided.

Both provide valuable evidence.

Records should explain:

- what happened;
- when and where it happened;
- what was affected;
- immediate action;
- available evidence;
- contributing conditions;
- root causes when understood;
- corrective action;
- follow-up responsibility;
- lessons for other processes.

The objective is learning and prevention, not blame.

Repeated events should trigger deeper review rather than repeated temporary correction.

## Review and Learning

Risks change as the project develops.

A risk should be reviewed when:

- new evidence becomes available;
- an incident or near miss occurs;
- a control changes or fails;
- equipment or software changes;
- production scale changes;
- a new supplier or process is introduced;
- regulations change;
- the review date is reached.

Reviews should confirm whether actions were completed and whether they reduced actual exposure.

Successful controls, failed controls and unexpected outcomes should all strengthen future decisions.

## Long-Term Direction

The long-term direction is a project in which uncertainty remains visible and decisions remain traceable.

Risks should be addressed early enough to protect people, food, knowledge and continuity.

Controls should remain proportionate, understandable and maintainable.

Experiments should remain possible.

Failures and near misses should become sources of learning.

Growth should occur only when the project can accept and manage the responsibilities it creates.

Risk management is successful when it supports safe progress, honest learning and a stronger food chain.
